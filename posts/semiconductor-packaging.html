From Chips to Chiplets

From discrete components to monolithic chips to disaggregated chiplets, the industry returns to modularity, but at an entirely different scale.

Modular Integrated Modular
Era I

Discrete Components

1950s onwards

Individual transistors, resistors, and capacitors soldered onto printed circuit boards. Each component optimized separately, but interconnects were slow, lossy, and physically large.

Shockley & the Transistor
William Shockley's transistor (Bell Labs, 1947) replaced vacuum tubes and launched the solid-state era. Within a decade, discrete transistors, resistors, and capacitors were being assembled onto printed circuit boards, creating the modern electronics industry.
1950s
William Shockley
1910–1989 · Transistor co-inventor
Co-invented the transistor at Bell Labs with Bardeen and Brattain. Nobel Prize 1956. Founded Shockley Semiconductor in Mountain View, whose defectors became Fairchild and later Intel.
Era II

Monolithic Integration

1960s onwards

Everything on a single silicon die. One process, one material, one set of design rules. Moore's Law drives relentless shrinking. From the first integrated circuits to today's advanced nodes, monolithic integration remains a powerful paradigm, but increasingly constrained by the "one process fits all" limitation at the leading edge.

Moore's Law
In 1965, Gordon Moore observed that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years. This exponential trajectory, enabled by ASML's lithography advances, drove four decades of performance gains and cost reduction, making monolithic integration the dominant paradigm.
1960s
Gordon Moore
1929–2023 · Moore's Law, Intel co-founder
Co-founded Intel in 1968 after Fairchild Semiconductor. His 1965 observation that transistor density doubles roughly every two years became the roadmap the entire industry followed for half a century.
Era III

System-on-Chip & the Scaling Wall

2000s onwards

SoCs push monolithic integration to its limits. Apple Silicon exemplifies the pinnacle: CPU, GPU, Neural Engine, and unified memory on one die. But ever larger dies mean skyrocketing design costs at advanced nodes, and not everything thrives on cutting-edge CMOS. Analog, RF, power, and photonics demand different materials and processes. The scaling wall is not a moment but an ongoing tension.

Dennard Scaling & the Foundry Model
Robert Dennard (IBM) showed that smaller transistors use proportionally less power. When that scaling broke down in the 2000s, rising fab costs made it impossible for most companies to own their own fabs. Morris Chang's TSMC (founded 1987) had already provided the answer: the pure-play foundry, enabling the fabless revolution that defines the SoC era.
2000s
Morris Chang
b. 1931 · TSMC founder, foundry model
Founded TSMC in 1987, inventing the pure-play foundry model. By separating chip design from manufacturing, he enabled the fabless revolution and made Taiwan the center of global semiconductor production.
Lisa Su
b. 1969 · AMD chiplet architecture pioneer
Became AMD CEO in 2014 when the company was near bankruptcy. Bet on chiplet architecture with Zen, proving that stitching small dies together could outperform monolithic designs at lower cost. Grew AMD's market cap from $2B to over $200B.
Era IV

Heterogeneous Integration

2010s onwards

Back to modular, but by design. Separately optimized chiplets in different materials and process nodes, assembled via advanced packaging: silicon interposers, hybrid bonding at μm-scale pitch, embedded bridges. Standards like UCIe enable multi-vendor interoperability. Packaging is no longer just a container; it is the system integration layer.

The Chiplet Bet
Lisa Su's AMD proved the chiplet model could beat monolithic giants. Zen 2 (2019) stitched together multiple small dies instead of one large one, delivering performance at a fraction of the cost. The approach has since been validated by Intel, Apple (M5 Fusion Architecture, 2026), and the UCIe standard for chiplet interoperability.
2010s
Cloudberry Insight
"Apple's M5 Fusion Architecture (March 2026) bonds two 3nm dies into a single SoC via advanced packaging. When a company like Apple shifts from monolithic to multi-die, the direction is clear. But the real opportunity goes beyond compute: photonics, interconnects, PICs, and CMOS can each be optimized on their own process and material, then integrated through advanced packaging. Startups no longer need to build a full chip. Build the best chiplet for your niche, whether that is a silicon photonic transceiver, a III-V laser, or a novel sensor, and packaging does the system integration. That changes the economics of deep tech investing."
